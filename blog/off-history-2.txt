# A Brief History of Offensive Language Detection - Part 2: The Transformer Revolution

*Published on 2024-02-15 | Reading time: ~10 minutes*

## Introduction

In Part 1 of this series, we explored the journey from traditional rule-based systems to deep learning approaches in offensive language detection. We saw how methods evolved from simple keyword matching to sophisticated neural networks like CNNs and LSTMs. However, the field was about to experience a paradigm shift that would revolutionize not just offensive language detection, but the entire landscape of natural language processing.


Welcome to Part 2, where we'll dive into the transformer architecture and the era of pre-trained language models that changed everything we thought we knew about processing text.


---

## The Transformer Revolution

Many of the limitations that were associated with previously popular architectures such as LSTMs were addressed with the introduction of the Transformer architecture, which resulted in a revolution in the domains of artificial intelligence, specifically in natural language processing. Transformer models, in comparison to other deep learning models such as recurrent neural networks (RNNs) and long short-term memory (LSTMs), make use of a self-attention mechanism. This mechanism enables the dynamic leveraging of various sections of the input without any loss of information, even for distant parts of the input.


This architecture has proven to be very useful for the detection of offensive language, which is a topic in which the context and the way words are used have an important impact when deciding whether or not a text is offensive. When it comes to the identification of offensive language, studies have demonstrated that transformer-based models perform better than LSTM, Bi-LSTM, and CNN-based techniques.



## Pre-trained Language Models and Transfer Learning

Using PLMs has become a highly effective method in natural language processing. It enables transfer learning and the utilization of knowledge gained from extensive pre-training on large text collections. Models like BERT and RoBERTa have shown great results in learning language representations from unstructured text data, showing in outstanding performance across a range of NLP tasks.


A self-supervised objective is typically used to train these models. Masking language modeling (MLM) is an example of this. In MLM, when considering a text input, certain tokens of that text are randomly selected and a mask is applied (i.e., the tokens are replaced by a special [MASK] token), and the model's goal is to predict the original tokens that were masked.


Another self-supervised objective utilized in models such as the BERT is the Next Sentence Prediction (NSP) objective in which objective involves deciding if a given sentence follows another sentence in the given text. Specifically, the model receives sentence pairs during training. Second sentences in the pair may be the actual following sentence in the text or random sentence from the corpus. The model has to figure out whether the second sentence follows the first or not.


### Pre-training

Pre-training is a technique that involves training a model on millions or billions of tokens in order to learn general language representations. This is done before the model is fine-tuned for specific tasks, which enables the model to make use of the knowledge that it has gained during the pre-training phase in order to improve its performance on specific tasks.


The process of supervised fine-tuning, which involves fine-tuning a pre-trained model on specified downstream tasks using labeled data, gained popularity as a result of the introduction of models such as BERT. As a result of fine-tuning these PLMs on specific datasets with task-specific classifiers, it is possible to adapt it to perform well in various downstream tasks. PLMs such as BERT and its variants have been successfully applied in a variety of natural language processing tasks, including text classification, question answering, and named entity recognition. Due to their strong performance in text classification tasks, several PLM models have been explored for offensive language detection tasks. BERT has been successfully fine-tuned for offensive language detection in various languages like English and other low resource languages like Arabic.


## Fine-tuning and Transfer Learning

As a result of the fact that PLMs are trained for general language tasks such as masked language modeling during the pre-training phase, they need to be fine-tuned before we can use them for any downstream task such as a text classification task. The most common method for this is called Vanilla Fine-tuning, and it consists of taking the weights of the pre-trained language model (PLM) and then adjusting them with task-specific data in order to learn the task representation.


In low-resource language environments, transfer learning has shown to be a promising method for offensive language identification. The process involves training a classification model on a language which is high resourced, like English, using a cross-lingual transformer model. Afterwards, the weights for a new language are initialized based on the saved weights from English.


One of the key developments in transfer learning for offensive language detection has been the introduction of cross-lingual transformer models, such as XLM-RoBERTa (XLM-R) which is a multilingual variant of RoBERTa. These models are pre-trained on large corpora covering multiple languages including low resource languages, enabling them to capture cross-lingual representations and facilitate knowledge transfer across languages. Studies demonstrate utilizing XLM-R model which is first trained on an English OLID Dataset and then the weights of the entire model are saved and used to initialize the weights for the target low-resource languages by testing on Hindi, Spanish and Bengali datasets which have shown effectiveness in offensive language detection for low-resource languages.


## Pre-fine tuning on intermedia tasks

Research has demonstrated that pre-finetuning pre-trained models such as BERT on an intermediate task before fine-tuning them on the downstream task can improve their performance in natural language understanding tasks. Pre-finetuning is an intermediate phase of learning that occurs after language model pre-training and before fine-tuning. This method allows the transfer of knowledge and representations from one task to another, particularly in situations where compositionality and generalization are essential.


According to studies, the results show that implementing a pre-finetuning approach for BERT models, generally leads to better performance in offensive language detection across various metrics. This demonstrates the effectiveness of this strategy in accurately identifying hate speech.

## The Impact and What's Next

The introduction of transformers and pre-trained language models marked a turning point in offensive language detection. These models didn't just improve performance metrics; they fundamentally changed how we approach the problem. The ability to capture long-range dependencies, understand context more effectively, and transfer knowledge across languages opened up new possibilities for content moderation at scale.


The success of BERT, RoBERTa, and their variants in offensive language detection tasks demonstrated that general language understanding could be successfully specialized for specific applications. This paradigm shift laid the groundwork for even more sophisticated approaches that we'll explore in our next part.


In Part 3, we'll examine how the evolution continued with Large Language Models (LLMs), exploring how models like GPT, T5, and their successors have pushed the boundaries even further, introducing new capabilities and challenges in offensive language detection.
